# Review: Multi-Round Revision Dynamics

**Reviewer**: Michael Bernstein (Stanford)
**Expertise**: Crowdsourcing, human computation, social computing
**Round**: 1
**Date**: 2026-02-05

---

## Overall Assessment

This paper presents an empirical analysis of multi-round revision dynamics across 14 papers subjected to AI-simulated expert review. The framing draws natural parallels to iterative crowd workflows—multiple reviewers provide structured feedback, the author revises, and the process repeats until quality thresholds are met. The P1/P2/P3 triage system is reminiscent of task decomposition in crowdsourcing, and the finding that 72% of improvement comes from P1 items aligns with known patterns in iterative refinement workflows.

The paper's strength lies in its process analysis rather than its AI component. The characterization of revision dynamics—diminishing returns, priority-driven effort allocation, convergence predictors—is genuinely useful regardless of whether the reviews come from humans or AI. However, the paper misses an opportunity to connect more deeply with the rich literature on iterative crowd workflows and human computation, where similar dynamics have been studied extensively.

My primary concern is ecological validity. The "reviewers" here are AI personas with no actual expertise or stake in the outcome. The dynamics of real peer review involve social factors (reputation, reciprocity, anchoring to other reviewers' scores) that are absent here. Without grounding in these realities, the findings risk being artifacts of the simulation rather than insights about revision dynamics.

## Major Issues (Blocking)

### M1: Missing Connection to Iterative Crowd Workflow Literature
The related work covers peer review studies and software engineering analogies but entirely misses the substantial body of work on iterative crowd workflows (Bernstein et al.'s Soylent, Little et al.'s iterative improvement, Dow et al.'s parallel vs. iterative feedback). These directly study the same phenomenon—multi-round quality improvement through structured feedback—and provide established frameworks the paper should engage with.

### M2: Ecological Validity of AI-Simulated Review Dynamics
The paper studies "revision dynamics" but the reviews are generated by AI personas, not real experts. Real peer review involves: (a) anchoring effects between reviewers, (b) reputation and social pressure, (c) genuine uncertainty about whether specific claims are true, (d) domain-specific knowledge that LLMs may lack. Without addressing how AI review dynamics compare to human review dynamics, the findings may not transfer. At minimum, the paper needs a calibration study.

### M3: No Analysis of Review Quality Across Rounds
The paper tracks *scores* across rounds but never examines whether *review quality* changes. Do later-round reviews contain more specific feedback? Do they identify different types of issues? Review quality dynamics are as important as score dynamics for understanding the iterative process.

## Minor Issues

### m1: Basecamp Module Under-Discussed
Basecamp (1 paper, 4 rounds) is the only dataset with more than 2 rounds, making it critical for the diminishing returns analysis. Yet it receives minimal attention. Dedicate a subsection to this case study.

### m2: Revision Strategy Section Lacks Process Detail
Section 5 analyzes revision *types* but not revision *process*. How long did revisions take? What was the author's strategy for addressing P1 items? This process data would strengthen the practical recommendations.

## Strengths

1. The P1/P2/P3 impact decomposition is a clean, actionable framework with clear empirical support
2. Convergence predictors (Section 4.4) provide practical guidance for budgeting review rounds
3. The paper is well-written and clearly structured, making its limitations transparent

## Questions for Authors

1. Have you compared the structure of AI-generated reviews to real venue reviews for similar papers? Do they exhibit similar issue distributions?
2. How does the P1/P2/P3 classification compare to established crowd workflow decomposition methods (e.g., find-fix-verify)?
3. Could the diminishing returns curve be partially explained by reviewer fatigue or reduced attention in later rounds, even for AI reviewers?

## Recommendations

- Add a Related Work section on iterative crowd workflows and connect the P1/P2/P3 framework to established decomposition methods
- Include analysis of review content quality across rounds, not just scores
- Add a calibration study comparing AI review dynamics to real venue dynamics for at least a subset of papers
- Expand the Basecamp 4-round case study as a detailed illustrative example
