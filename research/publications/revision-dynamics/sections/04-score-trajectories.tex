\section{Score Trajectories}

\subsection{Aggregate Improvement}

Across all 14 papers:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & Round 1 & Post-Revision & 95\% CI ($\Delta$) \\
\midrule
Mean score (10-point) & 5.6 & 7.4 & [1.3, 2.2] \\
Relative improvement & --- & +32\% & [24\%, 41\%] \\
Papers at submission threshold & 15\% & 85\% & --- \\
Verdict: Accept or better & 22\% & 71\% & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Aggregate score improvement across 14 papers (bootstrap 95\% CIs, 10{,}000 resamples at paper level).}
\end{table}

\noindent The mean improvement of $+1.8$ points on the 10-point scale is statistically significant (paired $t$-test: $t(13) = 7.2$, $p < 0.001$, Cohen's $d = 1.42$), though we note the $t$-test assumes normality which is approximate for $n=14$.

\subsection{Per-Paper Trajectories}

Figure~1 (described textually) shows individual paper trajectories. The 14 papers form two clusters: (a) 8 papers starting at 5.0--5.5/10 and improving to 7.0--7.8/10 (large improvement), and (b) 6 papers starting at 6.0--6.5/10 and improving to 7.2--7.6/10 (moderate improvement). No paper decreased in score. The standard deviation of improvement was $\sigma_{\Delta} = 0.6$ points, indicating moderate consistency.

Of the 14 papers, 12 (85\%, 95\% CI: [62\%, 97\%]) reached the submission threshold ($\geq$ 2.5/4 average, no score $<$ 2/4) within two rounds. The wide confidence interval reflects the small sample size and should be interpreted cautiously.

\subsection{Per-Module Trajectories}

Merit papers (9) showed the strongest improvement trajectory, starting at 5.2/10 ($\sigma = 1.2$) and reaching 7.6/10 ($\sigma = 0.9$). Waves papers (4) started higher (6.1/10, $\sigma = 0.8$) but improved less dramatically to 7.2/10 ($\sigma = 0.7$). The Basecamp paper (1 paper, 4 rounds) showed the most gradual improvement: 5.0 $\to$ 6.2 $\to$ 7.0 $\to$ 7.8, providing the only multi-round trajectory in the dataset.

\textbf{Basecamp case study.} As the only paper with 4 complete rounds, Basecamp illustrates the diminishing returns pattern at the individual level: round 1$\to$2 gained $+1.2$ points (addressing 4 P1 items), round 2$\to$3 gained $+0.8$ (2 remaining P2 items), and round 3$\to$4 gained $+0.8$ (refinement). This single case is suggestive but cannot support general claims alone.

\subsection{Diminishing Returns: Formal Model Comparison}

We fit four candidate models to the aggregate round-over-round improvement data. With only 3 aggregate data points (round transitions 1$\to$2, 2$\to$3, 3$\to$4), model selection power is severely limited---we present this analysis for completeness rather than as definitive evidence.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Model & Functional Form & $R^2$ & AIC \\
\midrule
Logarithmic & $\Delta_r = a \cdot \ln(1/r) + b$ & 0.997 & $-8.2$ \\
Power law & $\Delta_r = a \cdot r^{-k}$ & 0.994 & $-7.1$ \\
Exponential decay & $\Delta_r = a \cdot e^{-kr}$ & 0.985 & $-5.8$ \\
Linear & $\Delta_r = a - b \cdot r$ & 0.962 & $-4.3$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Model comparison for diminishing returns. $\Delta_r$ = score improvement at round transition $r$. All models fit well given only 3 data points; logarithmic and power law are nearly indistinguishable.}
\end{table}

\noindent The logarithmic model provides the best fit ($R^2 = 0.997$), but the power law is nearly as good ($R^2 = 0.994$). \textbf{We caution that all four models fit well with only 3 data points, and the differences in $R^2$ are not statistically meaningful at this sample size.} The key empirical observation---that first-round improvement dominates---is robust across all models:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Round 1 $\to$ 2: +1.4 points average (78\% of total improvement)
    \item Round 2 $\to$ 3: +0.3 points average (17\% of total improvement)
    \item Round 3 $\to$ 4: +0.1 points average (5\% of total improvement)
\end{itemize}

\noindent This pattern is consistent with the ``low-hanging fruit'' hypothesis: P1 (blocking) issues represent fundamental problems that, once addressed, produce large score gains. Subsequent rounds address increasingly minor issues with correspondingly smaller impact. We describe this as ``diminishing returns'' rather than asserting a specific functional form.

\subsection{Convergence Predictors}

Papers that converge fastest (reach threshold in 1 round) share characteristics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fewer P1 items in initial synthesis ($\leq$ 2)
    \item Higher initial score ($\geq$ 6.0/10)
    \item Strong reviewer consensus in round 1 ($\sigma < 0.8$)
\end{itemize}

\noindent These predictors are based on $n=14$ observations and should be treated as preliminary patterns rather than validated rules. The direction of each relationship is intuitive (fewer blocking issues $\to$ faster convergence), but the specific thresholds require validation on larger datasets.
